Quarry Field is an original example of an idle mining game built around preparing miners, choosing a route, and returning with materials for the next decision. This walkthrough explains the sample systems in a practical order. It makes no claims about the mechanics, rewards, or publisher of Bomb Farm or another real game.

Your first goal is a session you can understand and repeat. Learn where the important information lives, send a team to a comfortable map, and check its condition afterwards. Once that loop feels familiar, you can compare equipment, talent choices, and a rotation of three teams without losing track of what changed.

## The eight screens

The example interface groups decisions into eight screens. Use each screen to answer a specific question.

- **Home:** review the session state and decide what needs attention next.
- **Roster:** inspect miners, assign roles, and see which team is ready.
- **Equipment:** compare items and check who is currently wearing them.
- **Talents:** review the choices that shape each miner's contribution.
- **Maps:** choose a route suited to the active team's preparation.
- **Run:** follow the current expedition and its visible progress.
- **Inventory:** review materials and identify what an upgrade would consume.
- **Recovery:** check energy and decide which miners need more rest.

Begin with the roster and maps; keep equipment and recovery close at hand.

## The daily farming loop

Start by checking what is ready, not by selecting the hardest available route. Look at energy, confirm equipment assignments, and choose a team whose roles make sense together. In this example, three teams provide a useful way to organise the roster: one can run while another recovers and the third remains available. Rotation is a planning aid, not a requirement to send everyone out.

After a run, review the result before queueing the next decision. Check the team's condition, note any difficulty, and consider whether another ready team can continue comfortably. Spend materials on specific weaknesses. A reliable loop consists of preparation, a run, a review, and a deliberate next step.

## What happens during a run

A run turns your preparation into a visible expedition. The chosen map sets the context, while the team's roles, equipment, and talents shape how it approaches the route. Watch for signs that the team is comfortable or stretched. This sample does not assign universal timings or guaranteed rewards, so use comparable runs to learn what your particular setup can handle.

Avoid interpreting every difficult moment as a reason to rebuild the roster. Finish the review first, then identify the likely source of friction: map choice, a weak role, unsuitable equipment, or depleted energy. Record one hypothesis and change one thing for the next comparable run.

## Managing energy and recovery

Energy limits how much work a team can comfortably take on in a session. Check it before a run and again afterwards. A three-team rotation can give tired miners space to recover, but the rotation only works when the next team is actually ready. Switching the team label does not erase fatigue, and an unavailable roster is a reason to shorten the session. Do not assume a fixed timer or recovery item unless the interface explicitly supplies that information.

Plan recovery around your own schedule. Before leaving, note which team ran last, which equipment it holds, and what must be checked when you return. On your next visit, inspect the current state rather than trusting an old note as a guarantee. The rotation should keep decisions calm and visible.

## Building power gradually

Progress becomes easier to assess when each investment answers a concrete problem. Begin with the team you use most, identify its weakest contribution, and choose an affordable change that you can compare on a familiar route. Useful upgrades support the team plan.

- **Balance roles:** make sure the roster can support the route as a group.
- **Match equipment:** give useful items to miners who will actually use them.
- **Align talents:** favour choices that reinforce the team's current purpose.
- **Protect resources:** keep enough materials to make the next useful decision.

After the change, repeat a similar run and review the team's condition. If the result is still unclear, gather another observation before upgrading something else. Move to a harder map when the current route is comfortable and the next challenge has a clear purpose.

## A manageable session

Begin with a quick inspection: check the roster, energy, and equipment, then select a familiar route. Decide how much time you want to spend before starting. A small, complete session gives you a clearer result than an open-ended sequence of runs.

Complete the first run and pause for a review. If another team is ready and you still have time, continue the rotation. If the roster needs rest, finish by sorting materials and noting the next question. That preserves a clear starting place for next time.

End with a brief record of the active map, the last team used, and any planned change. Return to the <GuideLink slug="guides">guide overview</GuideLink> if you need to revisit the reading sequence. Your notes should make the next session easier to begin.

## Common mistakes

**Chasing difficulty too early.** A harder route is a poor comparison when you do not yet understand the familiar one. Establish a baseline before raising the challenge.

**Spreading every upgrade across the roster.** Small changes to unused miners may leave the active team struggling. Direct the next investment towards a problem you can observe.

**Rotating without checking readiness.** A team name is not an energy check. Inspect recovery and equipment before sending the next group.

**Changing everything after one run.** Several changes hide the reason for improvement or failure. Keep a brief note, test one adjustment, and let the result guide the next decision.
